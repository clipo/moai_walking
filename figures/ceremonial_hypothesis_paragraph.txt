To test these competing hypotheses, we developed a quantitative model of expected moai distributions under transport failure conditions. Our transport failure model predicts that failure probability should be highest near the quarry and decrease with distance, following established patterns in materials transport and engineering failure analysis. Critically, this model expects substantial stochastic variation because real-world transport failures involve random factors including variable structural integrity among moai, unpredictable weather and terrain conditions, differences in team experience, and inherent accident probability. We therefore predict: (1) early failures concentrated near Rano Raraku due to structural defects revealed under initial stress and learning-curve errors; (2) exponentially decreasing failure rates with distance as defective statues are eliminated and techniques improve, but with considerable variance around the trend; and (3) high overdispersion (variance >> mean) reflecting the stochastic nature of transport failures.

In contrast, a ceremonial placement model would predict either regular spacing intervals (marking territory or routes), clustering at culturally significant locations (viewsheds, boundaries, intersections), or increasing frequency at distances where quarry visibility is lost. We tested these predictions using comprehensive statistical analyses (Figure S1; see Supplemental Methods). 

Our analysis reveals overwhelming support for the transport failure model over ceremonial placement (Table S1). All four statistical tests support transport failure: 79.8% of road moai occur within 3.5 km of the quarry (the approximate viewshed boundary), the coefficient of variation (CV = 2.70) indicates strong clustering near the quarry rather than regular spacing, the exponential decay model is overwhelmingly preferred by AIC (100% model weight versus 0% for uniform or polynomial models), and critically, the moderate goodness of fit (R² = 0.52) combined with high overdispersion (variance/mean = 8.97) confirms the expected stochastic variation rather than deterministic placement. None of the four tests supported ceremonial placement predictions: we found no evidence of regular spacing (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p < 0.001), no meaningful clustering at specific distances (nearest-neighbor ratio = 0.80, indicating random pattern), no increased frequency at the viewshed boundary (density ratio = 1.05), and no statistical support for non-exponential models (Figure S2).

These results demonstrate that road moai distribution reflects the inherently stochastic nature of transport failure mechanics rather than deliberate ceremonial positioning. The moderate R² value, rather than weakening our conclusion, actually strengthens it by confirming that real-world transport involves the random variation expected from engineering failure processes, distinguishing it from either perfect mathematical models or regular ceremonial patterns.
